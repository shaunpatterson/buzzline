# Reading tools that change how text is presented: research for Buzzline

Researched 16 Nov 2026. Area: bionic bolding, RSVP and speed-reading apps, guided-reading aids (line focus, rulers, overlays), pacing aids and gaze-driven tools.

## Summary

Almost every "read faster" presentation trick has weak or negative independent evidence. Bionic-style bolding has at least five independent null or negative studies, and RSVP apps reliably cost comprehension because they remove regressions. The best-supported ideas in this area are the boring ones. Reading rulers and line focus have a 177-person controlled study showing speed gains for readers with dyslexia and no comprehension penalty. Imposed pacing that keeps the text on screen has a 110-person training study and a 2026 46-person study showing comprehension holds at speeds above a reader's natural rate (below about 405 wpm). For Buzzline, which already has gradient colouring and RSVP Sprint, the best borrows are (1) a line-focus ruler, (2) an in-page pacer that moves a highlight through the page text, and (3) regression-friendly controls for Sprint. Bionic bolding and coloured overlays should not be built as performance features. Gaze-driven rulers are interesting but too immature and heavy for now.

Evidence legend used below: **Strong** = replicated or preregistered controlled evidence of a benefit. **Moderate** = one decent controlled study (about 100+ participants) or several consistent small ones. **Weak** = small, vendor-run, unreviewed, or surrogate outcomes only. **None found** = I looked and found no independent study. **Negative** = independent studies show no benefit or harm.

Effort legend: **S** = about a day, **M** = about a week, **L** = more. "Fit" is browser-extension fit plus Buzzline's PDF reader (Original = gradient strips over a canvas; Reader = reflowed DOM text; per `src/pdf.js` header).

## 1. Pick 1: line focus / reading ruler

**What it is.** A band that marks the current line and dims or masks the neighbours, moved by pointer or arrow keys. Descends from the typoscope (card with a slot) and the tinted plastic ruler. Products: Immersive Reader Line focus (1/3/5 lines; [Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/education/onenote/use-line-focus-in-immersive-reader-for-office-for-the-web-and-onenote)), Helperbird Focus Ruler (free; height, colour, dim level; [Helperbird](https://www.helperbird.com/features/focus-ruler/)), ReadingRuler and about eight small extensions ([ReadingRuler listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/readingruler/llfjhgjhieplblmacijlmdfffocjhpbh?hl=en)).

**Evidence.** The only controlled study of digital rulers I found is Niklaus, Cai, Bylinskii and Wallace, *Digital Reading Rulers*, CHI 2023 ([paper PDF](https://thereadabilityconsortium.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Digital-Reading-Rulers-Evaluating-Inclusively-Designed-Rulers-for-Readers-With-Dyslexia-and-Without.pdf); [summary](https://readabilitymatters.org/articles/research-highlight-digital-reading-rulers)).

- **Who:** 313 crowdworkers recruited (Mechanical Turk and Prolific), 177 analysed: 91 with self-reported dyslexia plus screener score ≥13, and 86 without. Within-subject; each read passages with Grey Bar, Lightbox, Shade, Underline, mouse cursor, or no ruler. Reading speeds outside 50-650 wpm were dropped.
- **Result:** For readers with dyslexia, rulers changed speed (ANOVA p<0.01). Grey Bar beat no ruler (corrected p<0.01, Cohen's d 0.23). Grey Bar, Shade and Underline beat the mouse baseline (d 0.22-0.30). **No significant effect on comprehension.** Readers without dyslexia were on average fastest *without* a ruler, but preferred reading with one. Lightbox was the most preferred but not the fastest.
- **Strength:** moderate for speed in dyslexic readers; none for comprehension; remote, crowdsourced, 3-question comprehension checks, a fixed 500x480 px reading box, no scrolling. Authors say participants in their focus groups asked for keyboard control, word-level focus (60 participants) and eye tracking (24 participants).
- **GARB's abstract** (ETRA 2026) repeats the claim that rulers improve speed and comprehension for readers with and without dyslexia; the CHI paper itself found no comprehension effect, so treat that sentence as optimistic ([abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3797246.3806219), fetched via the Semantic Scholar API because the ACM page was blocked).

**Competitors' uptake.** The dedicated Chrome ruler extensions are tiny (5-805 users each). Line focus ships free inside Immersive Reader and Helperbird. That suggests rulers are expected to be a bundled feature rather than a product.

**Effort for Buzzline: S** for a pointer/arrow-key band with dim-the-rest and a few styles. Reason: one fixed overlay element plus a `mousemove`/key handler; no DOM rewriting. Snapping the band to real line boxes can reuse `textFragments()` in `src/content.js`, which already derives one box per line from `Range.getClientRects()` (about 2 extra days). **Fit:** works on any page. In the PDF reader's Original view it is easier than on the web because `src/pdf.js` already knows every line's `left/right/y/h`; in Reader view it is ordinary DOM. [INFERENCE] The ruler band and the gradient will both be drawing on the same line; they may need a rule (for example, gradient only inside the band, or ruler off while gradient is on).

## 2. Pick 2: in-page pacer ("Flow" / "Cruise")

**What it is.** A highlight (word or line) moves through the visible page text at a set WPM, optionally auto-scrolling. Unlike RSVP the surrounding text stays on screen, so the reader can still look back. Products: SwiftRead **Flow** ("keeps the full page visible and moves a highlight through it"; [Chrome listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/swiftread-read-faster-lea/ipikiaejjblmdopojhpejjmbedhlibno)), RSVP Reader **Cruise** ([vendor page](https://rsvpreaderapp.site/features/reading-modes)), Page Pacer ([listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/page-pacer-read-faster-st/oflnmeohkhahdmnmbemakhbgkfefbfnn): pace by WPM, auto-scroll, progress history, Pro $4.99/mo), Pacer speed reading ([listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/pacer-speed-reading/jcofnjnjaijcgkcdklddpokkdmdnmckn): open source, stops at paragraph end), AceReader (paces and auto-adjusts speed from comprehension tests; [AceReader](https://www.acereader.com/why-acereader)).

**Evidence.** No study of a moving-highlight pacer *product* found. The relevant research is about imposed pace in general:

- **Breznitz et al., Nature Communications 2013** ([article](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms2488)). 110 university students (55 dyslexic), Hebrew text. 24 sessions of 15-20 minutes. Sentences were erased letter by letter at a rate that started at each person's own pace and sped up or slowed by 2 ms per character on a staircase driven by comprehension answers. 40 per group trained with time pressure; 15 per group did the same sentences self-paced. Trained readers (typical and dyslexic) got faster and better on comprehension; the self-paced group got **no** gains; gains were retained at 6 months. Dyslexic comprehension during training rose 76% to 87%, typical 89% to 97%. Limitations: Hebrew, university adults, masking rather than a highlight, a lab training course.
- **Speed-accuracy study, Scientific Studies of Reading (2026)** ([abstract via Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1080/10888438.2025.2612649?fields=title,year,abstract)). 46 native-English students read at a range of speeds, "manipulated using a novel line-by-line method of text enhancement within a page". Comprehension stayed consistent across a broad range with declines only at 405 wpm; faster, higher word-efficiency readers held comprehension better at high speeds. I could read only the abstract, so the exact visual method is unverified.
- **Counterweight: Rayner et al. 2016** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26769745/)): there is a speed-accuracy trade-off; doubling or tripling speed (about 250 to 500-750 wpm) while understanding as well is unlikely; the way to read faster with good comprehension is to read more and build vocabulary.
- **A 2002 Chinese-text study** compared a smooth gliding-guide format with RSVP: the gliding guide gave better accuracy; speed and comprehension traded off ([journal abstract](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/xlxb/CN/Y2002/V34/I01/44), seen as a search snippet only).
- Normal silent reading for adult English non-fiction is 175-300 wpm per a 2019 meta-analysis of 190 studies as summarised by Reichle ([The Conversation, 3 Aug 2026](https://theconversation.com/some-speed-reading-apps-make-big-claims-but-how-fast-can-we-actually-read-287497)).

**Reading of the evidence.** Evidence is moderate that gentle pressure to read slightly faster than your habit, with the text still visible, can raise speed without losing comprehension for skilled adults, and that unassisted self-paced practice does not. Nothing shows a highlight pacer on a normal web page does this. A user-controlled WPM near their baseline is the safe default; the 405 wpm figure marks where comprehension fell in one study.

**Effort for Buzzline: M.** Reuse Sprint's `schedule.js` timings (word/punctuation holds) and the existing Range-highlight machinery; add a moving word/line highlight, auto-scroll-follow, pause/seek keys, per-site WPM. **Fit:** web yes; PDF Reader view yes (DOM); PDF Original view feasible at line level (line geometry exists) but not word level without more text-position data. Line-level pacing is the closest match to the 2026 study's "line-by-line" method.

**Warning.** Page Pacer's free tier caps pace at 250 wpm and 1 use a day and charges for uncapped speeds up to 3,000 wpm, which is the opposite of what the evidence supports. Do not offer unlimited WPM as a headline.

## 3. Pick 3: RSVP safeguards (Sprint)

Buzzline already ships RSVP with ORP, chunking and punctuation pauses, matching Sprint Reader, Spritz and Spreeder. The research here is about what RSVP costs.

- **Schotter, Tran and Rayner 2014**, *Psychological Science* ([abstract via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:24747167%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json)). A trailing-mask display (each word masked as the eye leaves it) mimicked RSVP by removing regressions. Comprehension fell, not only for ambiguous sentences. Conclusion: regressions support understanding; RSVP-style apps' viability is in question. Controlled lab study of adults; strong for the mechanism.
- **Benedetto et al. 2015**, *Computers in Human Behavior*: Spritz impaired literal comprehension and increased visual fatigue. I could only read a search snippet of the abstract, not the paper, so sample size and effect sizes are unverified. Reichle's summary of the same study says it found no difference in reading rate but impaired recall ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/some-speed-reading-apps-make-big-claims-but-how-fast-can-we-actually-read-287497)).
- **Acklin and Papesh 2017**, *American Journal of Psychology*: static text beat RSVP at 700 and 1,000 wpm; slower RSVP gave better verbatim recall. Seen only via secondary summaries ([Center for Inquiry](https://centerforinquiry.org/blog/does-speed-reading-improve-reading-comprehension/) as returned by search), not the paper.
- **Miyata et al. 2012**, *PLoS ONE* ([Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1371/journal.pone.0036091&resultType=core&format=json)): 17 participants including 3 speed-reading trainees; trainees read faster with lower comprehension; one expert had comparable but numerically lower comprehension.
- **Reichle's E-Z Reader simulation** ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/some-speed-reading-apps-make-big-claims-but-how-fast-can-we-actually-read-287497)): baseline 307 wpm; fixating each word's centre perfectly gives 318 (+3.6%); removing eye movements and attention shifts as RSVP does gives 429 (+39.7%) but predicts lower comprehension. One author's model, not an experiment.
- **Vendor claims** versus evidence: spritz.com says average readers read "around 200 wpm with a comprehension of 60%" and Spritz readers "300 wpm or more at a comprehension level of 90%", with no source on the page ([spritz.com](https://spritz.com/)). Rayner et al. found no support for the claim that large speed gains come free.
- No independent evidence found that highlighting the ORP letter helps in RSVP. The optimal-viewing-position literature is about isolated words; in sentence reading the preferred landing point is left of centre (search results on OVP; not read in full).

**Suggested safeguards (all [INFERENCE] from the evidence above):**

1. A "back one sentence" key that replays from the previous sentence start (a substitute for the regression RSVP removes).
2. A "context peek" that shows the surrounding sentence faded while paused.
3. A sentence-end hold longer than the comma hold (Buzzline already has configurable period/comma multipliers).
4. A first-run note that speeds above about 400 wpm are for skimming. The 2026 study's 405 wpm figure is for one population and method, so word this loosely.

**Effort for Buzzline: S.** Sentence boundaries are already implied by the period delay logic; the work is a key binding and a replay index. **Fit:** web and PDF Reader view yes (Sprint takes text); PDF Original view only through its extracted text.

## 4. Bionic Reading and other bold-the-start tools (not recommended)

**What it is.** Bold the first part of each word so the eye "fixates" on it and the brain "completes" the word. Owner: Bionic Reading AG (Chur, Switzerland). Registered trademark US 5,557,651 per Helperbird's reading of USPTO; the pricing page calls the method "patented" ([pricing](https://bionic-reading.com/br-pricing/)). I did not check patent scope. Have someone check before shipping a first-half-bold feature.

**Evidence, independent.**

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Readwise web experiment 2022 ([post](https://blog.readwise.io/bionic-reading-results/)) | 3,334 started, 1,916 analysed; two ~1,000-word Paul Graham essays, within-subject; unsupervised, not peer-reviewed | 325.3 wpm Bionic vs 327.9 plain; mean difference -2.6 wpm (p=0.055); comprehension 88% both |
| Snell 2024, *Acta Psychologica* ([abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.actpsy.2024.104304&resultType=core&format=json)) | 100 paragraphs, Bionic vs normal; 32 participants per a news write-up | No significant difference in reading times |
| Spear et al. 2025, *Attention, Perception & Psychophysics* ([paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-025-03067-w)) | Eye tracking, paragraphs, five bolding conditions | Bold-first-half did not beat bold-middle/last, and "led to costs relative to regular unbolded reading"; seeing only the first few letters was not enough to recognise words; not beneficial for any subgroup tested |
| Beelders 2025, *J. Eye Movement Research* ([abstract](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.3390/jemr18050049?fields=abstract)) | Eye tracking, standard vs Bionic font | No significant change in fixation durations, fixation counts or speed; fixations spread through the whole word; no help with low-frequency words |
| Možina et al. 2025, *SAGE Open* ([search result](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440251376158), page blocked) | Eye tracking, Verdana vs Georgia, students and middle-aged readers, screen and paper | Reading time **longer** with Bionic (search snippet) |
| Parker, Wang, Lee, Joseph 2026, *Royal Society Open Science*, registered report ([summary](https://scienceblog.com/bionic-reading-bolds-first-half-each-word-promise/), RSOS page blocked) | 90 skilled adults, single sentences in 18-pt Courier New, three conditions including a middle-bold control, Bayes-factor stopping rule | Bolding pulled the first fixation toward the start of 8-letter words; no change in gaze duration, total time, skipping or comprehension (Bayes factors <1/3); plain text rated most readable (43 of 90; start-bold 31; middle-bold 16) |

**Evidence, vendor-hosted.** ZHAW pilot 2022, hosted by the vendor ([page](https://bionic-reading.com/br-study/)): 12 applied-linguistics students; speed 191.28 vs 189.14 wpm (no significant difference, vendor says so); comprehension score 11.75 vs 9.83 (the vendor's headline "+19.5%"); more fixations on Bionic text, which ZHAW says may be chance; 58% found the treatment noticeable or "odd" at first. ZHAW called it a pilot with "several indications". The vendor says it does not claim a speed benefit.

**Secondary reports (not verified at source).** Helperbird, a competing extension vendor, summarises further studies ([page](https://www.helperbird.com/explained/bionic-reading/)): about 150 adults with dyslexia or suspected dyslexia, no improvement (CHI 2025); 490 Filipino tenth graders, no difference; Turkish sixth graders, improvement but post-test only; two 2026 second-language studies, one positive on comprehension and one null; 20 young people with learning disorders, more letters read correctly in a pilot. Treat as leads only.

**Why it should not work, and the ceiling.** Reichle's simulation gives about +3.6% for perfect landing on word centres. Skilled readers already skip about a third of words and use letters in parafoveal view; bolding moved only first-fixation position on long words (Parker 2026).

**Who likes it anyway.** Preference is real: the vendor and reviews cite ADHD and dyslexia readers saying it feels smoother. Preference is not performance. The vendor's own Chrome extension is rated 2.4★ by 320 raters despite 100,000 installs.

**Effort for Buzzline: M.** Reason: the CSS Custom Highlight API accepts only `color`, `background-color`, `text-decoration`, `text-shadow` and `-webkit-text-stroke-*` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/::highlight)), not `font-weight`. Real bolding therefore needs wrapping word prefixes in elements, which [INFERENCE] fights Buzzline's Range-based colouring that keeps text nodes stable. A `-webkit-text-stroke-width` stand-in is possible but I have not tested how it looks. **Fit:** web yes (via DOM wrapping); PDF Reader view yes; PDF Original no (canvas).

**Recommendation.** Do not build as a performance feature. If ever added, make it an off-by-default "emphasis style" with honest copy.

## 5. Gaze-driven tools (later, not now)

- **GARB** (Nyamekye and Tregubov, ETRA 2026): browser-based real-time eye tracking that moves line and word highlights; modes gaze-driven, fixed-pace, manual, none; evaluation **planned**, pilot only showed logging works ([abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3797246.3806219)).
- **GazePrompt** (CHI 2024): 13 low-vision readers; reduced line-switching time and word errors, improved experience; a silent-reading follow-up showed better concentration and perceived comprehension ([abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3613904.3642878), via Semantic Scholar).
- **Yang, Yan, Du** (arXiv 2409.19454 v3): gaze error about 1.9 cm (typical 2-3 cm) against 3-5 mm line spacing, so they use reading-order assumptions plus an LLM to re-locate after jumps; 84% accuracy on jump reading in controlled tests; field test with 18 volunteers cut paragraph reading time by 13.5% versus baselines; Tobii hardware ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19454v3)).
- **WebGazer.js**: webcam gaze that self-calibrates from clicks and cursor, runs client-side; GPLv3 with an LGPLv3 option for companies valued under $1M; "official maintenance has ended" as of 24 Feb 2026 ([GitHub](https://github.com/brownhci/WebGazer)).
- **GazeScroll**: Chrome extension that scrolls when you look down; its page was last modified in Nov 2019 ([page](https://gazerecorder.com/gazescroll/)).

**Verdict.** Eye-tracked rulers are the logical successor to pick 1 (24 participants in the CHI ruler study asked for eye tracking), but webcam accuracy is far coarser than a line, needs a camera permission, a calibration flow, GPL-licensed code and, per Yang et al., language-model help to stay correct. **Effort: L.** Revisit after the manual ruler exists and only if users ask.

## 6. Guided reading: gradients and line-start bolding (context for the existing feature)

Buzzline's gradient is BeeLine-style, so the evidence matters even though it is not new.

- **Koornneef and Kraal 2022**, *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* ([abstract](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/3448262?rep=01&m=9940749629002711)): two experiments with second- and third-grade pupils (6-9 years). BeeLine gave a reading-time advantage for second graders only with long lines and little line spacing. With layouts optimised for beginners (short lines, ample spacing) second graders read **slower** with BeeLine. BeeLine may hamper comprehension for third graders, and texts were rated harder and less convenient. Conclusion: "may be useful for some beginning readers in some situations" but can impede readability. Children only; no independent adult study found.
- **Slattery and Vasilev 2019** ([paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-019-01742-3)): 32 adults read two short stories, one with the first word of every line in bold. Return sweeps landed 0.54 characters closer to the line start and undersweeps were less likely, **but page reading time (23.3 vs 23.1 s) and comprehension (96.2% vs 97.5%) did not differ.** The authors suggest skilled adults already benefit from information gathered at undersweep fixations, and that children or dyslexic readers might gain more (speculation).
- **Vendor figures** on beelinereader.com: in Perlego's testing college students using BeeLine spent 69% more time reading and in a CNET trial users scrolled about 50% further; these are engagement measures, not comprehension, and I could only see them on the vendor's page ([page](https://www.beelinereader.com/)). The Atlantic piece they cite describes an earlier small non-peer-reviewed study by an optometrist for the company (search snippet; page blocked).
- **Chrome listing** claims "many people are able to read 20% or 30% faster" and "independent, educator-led research" shows gains. I did not find the research behind it ([listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/beeline-reader/ifjafammaookpiajfbedmacfldaiamgg)).

**Implication.** The gradient's theory (help the eye land on the next line) is plausible, and the best evidence on return sweeps shows less oculomotor error but no speed gain in skilled adults. Keep the gradient as a preference with honest copy, not as a "reads faster" claim. A line ruler (pick 1) has stronger direct evidence.

## 7. Coloured overlays and Irlen-style tints (not recommended as a performance feature)

- **Griffiths et al. 2016**, systematic review of 51 items ([abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1111/opo.12316&resultType=core&format=json)): most studies at high or uncertain risk of bias; where benefits were reported the effect was small and similar to placebo; the authors conclude use "cannot be endorsed" and benefits are likely placebo, practice or Hawthorne effects.
- **Suttle et al. 2018**, overview of four reviews ([abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1111/cxo.12676&resultType=core&format=json)): three of four found insufficient evidence; "not yet a reliable evidence base on which to recommend".
- RANZCO's 2018 position statement says there is no scientific evidence that Irlen syndrome exists or that Irlen lenses work (search snippet; PDF fetch failed).

A tint is trivially cheap (**S**), and some people like it. Offer it, if at all, as a comfort setting, not as treatment.

## Top three picks for Buzzline

1. **Line focus / reading ruler (S).** It has the best direct evidence in this area (CHI 2023, n=177, speed gains for dyslexic readers, no comprehension cost, and both groups preferred rulers to no ruler), it is cheap, it works on every page and on both PDF views, and it complements rather than copies the gradient. Build the pointer plus arrow-key band with Grey Bar, Shade, Lightbox and Underline styles, because the study found no single style wins and rulers designed with dyslexic readers did best. Add line-snapping from `textFragments()`.
2. **In-page pacer, "Flow" (M).** It brings RSVP's pacing benefit without RSVP's cost, since text stays visible and regressions stay possible. Evidence is moderate and indirect (Breznitz 2013; the 2026 study), and rivals (SwiftRead Flow, RSVP Reader Cruise, Page Pacer) validate demand but not efficacy. Reuse `schedule.js` timings and the Highlight API; let the user set WPM near their own habit and step it up slowly; do not market unlimited speed.
3. **Sprint safeguards (S).** Add back-one-sentence, a context peek and a stronger sentence-end hold, grounded in Schotter 2014 and the comprehension losses reported for Spritz-style apps. It makes Buzzline's existing RSVP defensible rather than adding another mode.

Not recommended: Bionic bolding (negative evidence, trademark/patent question, `::highlight()` limitation, M effort), coloured overlays as treatment, and gaze tracking for now.
