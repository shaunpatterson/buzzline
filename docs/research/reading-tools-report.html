Buzzline research · 16 November 2026

What else could Buzzline add?

I surveyed reading apps, devices, browser extensions and research studies for ideas worth borrowing. Three stand out, and a few popular ones are best left alone.

The top three: a line-focus ruler, a text-and-spacing panel for the Reader, and an end-of-read recall prompt. Each has real research behind it, needs no server or AI model, and fits Buzzline's existing pieces.

The honest headline: almost every "read faster" trick has weak or negative independent evidence. The better-supported ideas are the plain ones: help the eye keep its place, let people tune the text, and make them recall what they read.

Skip: bionic bolding, tinted overlays as a treatment, AI chat about the text, and eye tracking. Details and reasons are in the "Don't build" section.

The top three

Ranked by evidence first, then effort, then how well each fits what Buzzline already is.

1

Line-focus ruler

A band that marks the current line and dims the rest. Move it with the mouse or arrow keys.

Evidence: moderateEffort: smallWeb pages and both PDF viewsPicked by 2 of 3 research passes

Why

The best-supported idea in the presentation-aids area. In a controlled study of digital rulers (Niklaus et al., CHI 2023), 177 adults were analysed, 91 of them with dyslexia. For readers with dyslexia the grey-bar ruler beat no ruler on speed (effect size 0.23), with no loss of comprehension. Readers without dyslexia were fastest with no ruler, but preferred reading with one.

Microsoft's Immersive Reader and Helperbird already ship it free, so people expect it as a built-in feature.

How it would work in Buzzline

  • A fixed overlay moved by pointer or keys, with a few styles (grey bar, shade, lightbox, underline), since the study found no single style wins.
  • Snapping to real lines can reuse the line-box code already in content.js (textFragments()), about two more days.
  • In the PDF reader's Original view it is easier than on the web: pdf.js already knows every line's position.
Caveats: the speed gain was shown for readers with dyslexia only. The study was online and crowdsourced, with three-question comprehension checks. Whether a ruler and the gradient should be on together is untested; my guess is the gradient should show only inside the band.
2

Text-and-spacing panel for the Reader

Font choice, size, letter, word and line spacing, and column width, set by the reader.

Evidence: moderate, mixedEffort: small to mediumReader overlay and PDF Reader view

Why

It is the one area backed by several peer-reviewed experiments, and the Reader overlay plainly lacks it: I checked, and it is fixed at 16px with 1.7 line height and no controls. (The PDF reader got size buttons earlier, but not spacing or width.)

The evidence supports user control over a fixed preset: a study of personalisation (Sheppard and Joo) found the best setting differs from reader to reader.

What the research says

  • Spacing: extra-large spacing helped children with dyslexia in one study, replications only partly agree. Wider letter spacing without wider word spacing slowed reading in another.
  • Line length: shorter lines helped some readers and not others.
  • Dyslexia fonts: no benefit. A 2026 meta-analysis of 15 studies (688 people) found an effect of −0.04.
Design rules from the evidence: keep word spacing at least as large as letter spacing; offer fonts as a choice and never claim one helps; leave the defaults alone.
3

End-of-read recall prompt

After Sprint finishes or the Reader closes: "What were the main points?" A free-recall box, then the text revealed.

Mechanism: strong evidenceEffort: mediumNo account, server or AIPage text and PDF Reader view

Why

Testing yourself beats rereading: across many lab studies the effect is 0.50 (Rowland 2014). For prose, rereading won after five minutes, but after two days and a week the tested group recalled substantially more (Roediger and Karpicke 2006), while the rereaders felt more confident.

It also targets a known screen weakness: people reading on screens judge their own understanding less accurately than people reading on paper (Clinton 2019).

How it would work in Buzzline

  • Opt-in, one prompt long. Then reveal the passage, or a version with one key word per sentence hidden (no AI needed).
  • Reuses the text Buzzline already extracts for Sprint and the Reader.
  • Leave scheduling and spaced review for later.
Caveats: this is the least proven as a product. In one course experiment about 80% of people offered review questions never touched them, and grades did not differ. No study tests a prompt like this on arbitrary web pages. Measure how many people finish it before building more.

Don't build

Popular, but the evidence or the economics say no.

Bionic bolding

Independent studies find no speed or comprehension gain: at least five null or negative results, including a 90-person registered report in 2026. The only positive result is a 12-person pilot hosted by the vendor. The vendor calls the method patented, which someone should check before any first-half-bold feature. Technically it also cannot be done with the highlight API Buzzline uses.

Tinted overlays as a treatment

A 2016 review of 51 items concluded use "cannot be endorsed" and that benefits look like placebo. Fine as a comfort setting if ever offered; never as therapy.

AI chat and summaries

A 2025 study of 10,462 people found learning from AI summaries was shallower. Kindle, Readwise and Chrome already ship this. It would add cost, keys and privacy review. The opposite idea, making the reader produce something, is item 3.

Eye tracking

Webcam gaze is far coarser than a line of text, needs camera permission and calibration, and the main library's maintenance ended in February 2026 (GPLv3 licence). Revisit only if people ask after the ruler exists.

What this says about what Buzzline already has

Worth knowing, because the same research covers the existing features.

The line gradient

The only peer-reviewed study of this kind of gradient (Koornneef and Kraal 2022) tested children aged 6 to 9 and found mixed results, including slower reading on well-spaced layouts. In adults, bolding the first word of each line cut return-sweep errors but not reading time (Slattery and Vasilev 2019). BeeLine's positive figures are its own. Suggestion: describe the gradient as a preference, not as "reads faster".

Sprint (one word at a time)

Studies of this style (Schotter 2014, and others on Spritz) find comprehension drops because it removes look-backs (Schotter 2014). Separately, static text beat one-word-at-a-time reading at 700 and 1,000 words per minute (Acklin and Papesh 2017, seen only through secondary summaries). Buzzline's Sprint matches the leading products' features, so there is nothing to add except the safeguards above, and an honest note that very high speeds are for skimming.

Read aloud

Already there at paragraph level. The research gives little reason to prioritise more: audio helps little when you control the pace. It may matter more for Sprint, which is machine-paced (my inference).

What I could not verify

  • Several publisher pages blocked automated access (Wiley, ScienceDirect, ACM, SAGE, Mozilla, others). For those the reports used abstracts, PubMed or Crossref records, PDF copies or write-ups, and mark each one.
  • No independent study of any pacer product, or of a recall prompt on ordinary web pages, was found.
  • Prices for Matter, Raindrop Pro, Cold Turkey and Voice Dream come from third-party sites. User counts and ratings were as shown on 16 November 2026 and change.
  • Patent and trademark claims (Bionic Reading, BeeLine, Spritz) come from vendor pages; I did not read the patents.
  • Whether Chrome's Google voices have gained word-boundary events since mid-2024 is untested.
  • Whether Buzzline works on Boox e-ink browsers, or how its gradient looks in grayscale, is untested.
