# Colour schemes for a line-gradient reading aid: what the research supports

Researched 14 November 2026 for Buzzline. Scope: which colours suit a line-gradient reading aid, and whether the evidence supports adding a scheme.

## Summary

Published evidence that line-gradient colouring improves reading is thin: the only peer-reviewed experiment found (children aged 6–9, black–blue–red gradient) reports a speed gain for second-graders on crowded layouts, a slowdown on well-spaced layouts, no comprehension gain and lower ease-of-reading ratings, and the positive results BeeLine Reader cites are unpublished or first-party summaries [7][4][5]. No study compares one gradient palette with another, so nothing published shows that any scheme reads better than another. What the wider literature does support is that legibility follows luminance contrast rather than hue [14][15][16][17], so Buzzline's existing 4.5:1 rule matters more than which hues are chosen, and that hue pairs differ in how well they survive colour-vision deficiency [33]. On that basis one extra scheme is defensible, a blue/orange pair taken from Okabe and Ito's colour-universal palette, with a grey-only scheme as an optional second; a third (teal/amber) rests on weak, indirect evidence. A dichromat simulation computed for this note predicts that `ocean` is the weakest current scheme for colour-deficient readers and that the blue/red schemes (`classic`, `highcontrast`) hold up better than their hue names suggest.

## How to read this note

- Numbers in square brackets refer to the source list at the end. Every source listed there was opened on 14 November 2026 unless it appears under "Sources that could not be opened or verified".
- For sources linked to PubMed, only the PubMed record (title, authors, abstract) was read unless the entry says "full text". Claims drawn from them are limited to what the abstract states.
- **Inference** marks a conclusion of this note that no source states directly.
- **Computed** marks numbers calculated for this note (method in section 3 and under "Candidate schemes"). They are not published findings.
- Evidence labels: *strong* = systematic reviews or several controlled studies that agree; *moderate* = at least one peer-reviewed controlled experiment; *weak* = a single small or indirect study, or work that is not peer-reviewed; *first-party* = the vendor's own description, not independently checked.

## 1. Does line-gradient colouring help reading at all?

**The mechanism it targets is real.** The eye movement from the end of one line to the start of the next (a return sweep) often lands short of the line start and needs a corrective saccade [10][11]. In one eye-tracking study 57% of line-initial fixations were such "undersweeps" [9]. Return sweeps affect about 20% of all reading fixations [11]. Children need corrective saccades more often than adults (47 adults and 48 children reading the same texts) [10]. Evidence: moderate.

**Making the line start more salient reduces that error but did not make adults faster.** Slattery and Vasilev (2019; 32 adults, eye tracking; full text read) put the first word of every line in bold. Return sweeps landed 0.54 characters closer to the margin and undersweeps became less likely, but page reading time did not change (23.1 s normal, 23.3 s bold) and comprehension did not differ. The authors suggest skilled adults already use the information picked up during an undersweep, and say it is unknown whether children or dyslexic readers would benefit [9]. This is the closest controlled test of the idea behind a line cue, and it used weight, not colour. Evidence: moderate, one study.

**The one peer-reviewed test of gradient colouring is mixed.** Koornneef and Kraal (2022; full text read) ran two experiments with Dutch second- and third-graders (174 and 97 pupils) using BeeLine's default black–blue–red gradient against black text, measuring reading time per word, comprehension questions and ratings [7]:

- Second-graders read gradient text faster when lines were long and line spacing tight, and slower when the layout was already suited to them (short lines, extra spacing). Third-graders showed no clear speed effect [7].
- Comprehension did not improve. One comparison showed lower comprehension for third-graders, which the authors treat cautiously as a possible false positive [7].
- Children rated gradient text as less convenient and more difficult, with weaker trends for enjoyment and self-rated understanding [7].
- Limits stated by the authors: no eye tracking, so the return-sweep mechanism was not tested directly; no training; short static texts; only typically developing beginners; only the black–blue–red setting [7].
- They note that before their study there were "no published peer-reviewed studies on how BeeLine Reader affects the readability of texts and the eye-movement behaviour of readers", and conclude the claim that it "improves reading ability for students of all ages and skill levels" is not supported by their data [7].

A PubMed search for "BeeLine Reader" on the research date returned no records, and web searches found no peer-reviewed study in adults, in dyslexic readers, or with eye tracking.

**Expert criticism.** Rayner and colleagues' review of speed-reading methods (2016; relevant section read) says of BeeLine that "return sweeps are not the complicated part of reading … language processing is", so "any savings in reading time provided by making return sweeps more efficient may constitute only a minimal improvement" [8]. They cite no data on BeeLine itself. An earlier commentary noted that BeeLine's speed figures came from an internal analysis (60% of readers faster, about 20% average gain), had not been independently researched or peer reviewed, and could reflect expectation effects; its author disclosed a prior working relationship with BeeLine's founder [13].

Whether a colour gradient makes anyone read faster or understand more is unproven for adults and mixed for children [7]. Among the sources found, line-skipping errors as an outcome were measured only in an unpublished study [12].

## 2. Which colours?

No source found compares gradient palettes with each other. The following is what neighbouring research says.

### 2.1 Luminance contrast versus chromatic contrast

- Reading speed in normal vision is tolerant of reduced luminance contrast: for 1° letters it fell by less than a factor of two for a tenfold contrast reduction, with similar results for white-on-black and black-on-white; high contrast matters most for low-vision readers [14]. Evidence: moderate (psychophysics, small samples).
- Text defined by colour contrast alone can be read as fast as high-luminance-contrast text (above 300 words per minute) when the colour contrast is high; when both are present they do not add, and the better of the two sets the reading rate (8 normal and 10 low-vision subjects). Low-vision subjects gained nothing from colour contrast [15].
- Once 0.12 luminance contrast is present, adding chromatic contrast makes no difference to reading rate across a 30-fold range of character sizes [16].
- At matched luminance, the wavelength of text (blue, green, red or grey) did not change reading rate for normally sighted observers except near the acuity limit; where wavelength mattered at all, performance tended "to be depressed either in the red or the blue and to be nearly optimal for green or gray" [17].
- Twenty adults reading 2-minute passages in fourteen text/background colour combinations showed no significant difference in reading speed; the low-luminance-contrast pair was rated least legible [18].
- In a legibility ranking of 56 colour combinations by 308 participants on LCDs, the best were high-contrast, dark-on-light pairs [19].
- A review of low-vision work concluded chromatic contrast does not aid low-vision reading [57].
- WCAG's contrast requirement is built on the same finding: for people with colour-vision deficiency "hue and saturation have minimal or no effect on legibility as assessed by reading performance", so contrast "is calculated in such a way that color (hue) is not a key factor" [21]. The 4.5:1 figure is the 3:1 minimum for normal observers multiplied by 1.5 for the contrast-sensitivity loss associated with 20/40 acuity; 7:1 corresponds to 20/80 [21].

**Inference:** once every colour meets 4.5:1 against its surface, as Buzzline already enforces, the choice of hue is not expected to change reading speed for normally sighted readers. Hue choice then matters for two other things: whether the line cue is visible to all readers (section 3) and comfort (2.2). Readers with low vision are better served by more luminance contrast than by any hue choice [14][15][57].

One further point bears on how the cue is seen. Colour vision resolves less fine detail than luminance vision: above about 0.5 cycles per degree, contrast sensitivity is greater for luminance gratings than for red-green or blue-yellow ones, and acuity based on colour differences alone is limited to 11–12 cycles per degree [29]. Okabe and Ito make the practical version of this point: colours of thin lines and small characters are hard to tell apart, and thicker strokes help [33]. **Inference:** for body text, part of the anchor-versus-accent cue is carried by the lightness difference between them, not only by hue, so schemes whose accents differ clearly in lightness from the anchor are safer.

### 2.2 Saturated red and blue together

- **Chromatic aberration is real and sizeable.** The eye's focus differs by about 1.82 dioptres between 420 nm and 660 nm (20 subjects) [22]; a standard optical model of the eye reproduces both the longitudinal and transverse forms [23]. **Inference:** saturated blue and saturated red therefore cannot both be in sharpest focus at once.
- **Chromostereopsis is real but varies between people.** Red and blue on the same plane can appear at different depths. The effect comes from transverse chromatic aberration interacting with pupil position and size [25][28]. With natural pupils roughly equal numbers of people see red in front and blue in front, and for about half of 30 subjects the direction reversed when illumination changed [24]. It appears with stimuli from the two ends of the spectrum [26] and has been demonstrated with red and blue display primaries on a black background [27]. Evidence that the illusion exists: strong. 
- **Evidence that either effect harms reading: none found.** The reading studies above found no speed cost for red or blue text at normal sizes [17][18]. One study found a blue/red text-background pairing produced a heightened accommodative (focusing) response during reading, without a change in reading speed [18]. Evidence: weak.
- In Buzzline's four-stop layout (line *i* runs from colour *i* to colour *i*+1 of `[anchor, accentA, anchor, accentB]` [58]) a line fades between the anchor and one accent, so the two accents never meet on the same line and are separated vertically by an anchor-coloured line end. **Inference:** the conditions that produce strong chromostereopsis (large saturated red and blue areas side by side) do not arise in normal use, so this is a comfort argument at most, and a weak one.
- Koornneef and Kraal speculate that red in particular may distract young readers and that BeeLine's non-red settings "may be more effective"; they did not test this [7].

### 2.3 How many distinct hues

- No experiment varies the number of hues in a gradient scheme.
- The patent's basic form uses two anchor colours and a two-line cycle; it adds that more anchors allow "many of the anchor colors to be black", for example red, black, blue, black, so that "roughly half of the output text would be a blackish color" [1]. This is the layout Buzzline uses. The stated reason is familiarity, not measured performance [1].
- Colour boundaries placed inside words slow reading: colouring the two halves of each word differently increased reading time relative to single-colour text, while giving each whole word its own colour was read faster than single-colour text (children and adults, with and without dyslexia) [32]. **Inference:** this argues for gradual transitions and few hues, which a two-accent gradient already satisfies; it does not show that two accents are better than one or three.
- **Inference:** from the layout itself, the comparison a reader's eye must make at each return sweep is accent versus anchor (adjacent line starts alternate between them). The two accents only distinguish a line from the line two below it. A scheme therefore loses little if its two accents look alike, and loses a lot if an accent looks like the anchor.

### 2.4 Does the neutral anchor matter?

- No study tests the anchor colour of a gradient scheme.
- The patent's argument for black anchors is that "users are likely most accustomed to reading black text" [1]. The children in Koornneef and Kraal's study preferred plain black text, which the authors suggest may be a familiarity effect [7].
- A black (or, on dark pages, white) anchor has the highest luminance contrast available, which is what reading performance tracks (section 2.1) [14][19]. **Inference:** a pure black or white anchor also maximises the lightness gap to the accents, which is the part of the cue that survives colour-vision deficiency (section 3).
- On dark surfaces WCAG's rationale singles out one case: "predominantly long wavelength colors against darker colors" lose effective contrast for people with protanopia, hence its advice to avoid red on black [21]. Okabe and Ito say the same: avoid red characters on dark backgrounds, and prefer vermilion (a yellowish red) to pure red [33].
- Background tint affects reading speed: with black text on ten background colours (341 participants, 89 with dyslexia), warm backgrounds (peach, orange, yellow) were read faster and cool ones (blue, blue-grey, green) slower, in both groups. The study did not include white or off-white and the authors do not turn the result into a recommendation [56]. This concerns the page, which Buzzline does not control, not the text colour.

## 3. Colour-vision deficiency

**How common.** Inherited red-green deficiency affects about 8% of men and 0.4% of women of European descent, and 4–6.5% of men of Chinese and Japanese ethnicity [36]. Blue-yellow (tritan) deficiency affects fewer than 1 in 10,000 people [37].

**What is confused.** For red-green deficient viewers, Okabe and Ito list as hard to tell apart: red and green of similar brightness; yellow-green and yellow; blue and violet; green and brown; red and brown; and low-saturation pairs generally. Protanopes also see red as dark, so dark red is close to black. These viewers discriminate bluish hues well and rely more on brightness and saturation differences [33]. Tritan viewers have trouble separating blue from green, and dark blue from black [33][37]. Protanopes and deuteranopes showed better achromatic (light-dark) contrast sensitivity than controls in one study of 36 men [38], and luminance contrast perception is not impaired by colour deficiency according to WCAG's rationale [21].

**A palette built for this.** Okabe and Ito's set of colours "unambiguous both to colorblinds and non-colorblinds" is, in RGB: black (0,0,0), orange (230,159,0), sky blue (86,180,233), bluish green (0,158,115), yellow (240,228,66), blue (0,114,178), vermilion (213,94,0), reddish purple (204,121,167) [33]. Their rules for combining them: alternate warm and cool colours; when two are both warm or both cool, make them differ clearly in brightness or saturation; for thin lines and small objects prefer the darker blue and orange to sky blue and yellow [33]. Its authors are themselves protanopes, and the palette is guidance from practice rather than the result of a published discrimination experiment on that page [33]. Evidence that these colours stay distinct for dichromats: moderate (the authors' figure shows each colour under protan, deutan and tritan simulation [33], and the computation below agrees for the pairs used here); evidence that they help reading: none.

**Simulation models.** Brettel, Viénot and Mollon (1997) project colours onto the reduced colour surface of each dichromat type, and confirmed the match with a protanope and a deuteranope [35]. Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) give a model covering normal vision, anomalous trichromacy and dichromacy, validated with colour-deficient participants, and publish ready-made RGB matrices by severity [34]. Okabe and Ito caution that such simulations show the most strongly affected viewers, that many colour-deficient people see more than the simulation suggests, and that a simulated image is not literally what they see [33].

## 4. Dark pages and night reading

- **Light-on-dark text is somewhat harder to read, because the display is dimmer.** Dark text on a light background gave better acuity and proofreading in younger and older adults [39], and the advantage grew as characters got smaller [40]. When overall display luminance was equalised the advantage disappeared, so polarity itself is not the cause [41]. The proposed mechanism is that a brighter screen constricts the pupil, which reduces optical blur; legibility was worst for light-on-dark text in dark surroundings [42]. Older drifting-text psychophysics found reading rates "very similar" for the two polarities [14]. Evidence: moderate, with some disagreement between methods.
- **Inference:** because pupils are larger when viewing dark pages [42], the eye's chromatic aberration [22] has more effect there, so accents from the two ends of the spectrum (saturated blue, saturated red) are the likeliest to look soft on dark surfaces. No study tests this for text colours.
- **Red on dark** loses contrast for protan viewers [21][33]; see section 2.4.
- **"Halation" (light text appearing to glow or smear on dark backgrounds, said to affect people with astigmatism).** No primary study measuring this was found. The search surfaced blog posts and one paywalled 2026 book chapter that asserts it in its abstract. Treat it as unverified. The pupil-size mechanism above [42] is the nearest supported explanation for blur on dark pages.
- **Blue light and sleep.** Melatonin suppression is most sensitive to light of 446–477 nm [43], and reading a light-emitting e-reader before bed delayed sleep and suppressed melatonin compared with a printed book [44]. But shifting a screen's colour toward warm without lowering its brightness did not significantly change melatonin suppression [45], a randomised study of 167 young adults found no difference in sleep outcomes between Night Shift on, Night Shift off and no phone [46], and systematic reviews of blue-filtering spectacles found no good evidence of benefit for eye strain, visual performance or sleep [47][48]. Evidence that screen light in the evening affects sleep: moderate. Evidence that changing colour balance alone fixes it: weak to negative.
- **Inference:** on a dark page the text is a small share of the light the screen emits, and the evidence above points to brightness rather than colour balance. There is no research basis for a "night" scheme that claims a sleep or eye-strain benefit from avoiding blue accents.

## 5. Dyslexia and visual stress

- **Tinted overlays and lenses are not supported as a treatment for reading difficulty.** A systematic review of 51 publications found most studies at high or uncertain risk of bias, smaller effects in the better-controlled studies, and benefits generally small or similar to placebo; it concludes their use "cannot be endorsed" [49]. An overview of four systematic reviews found three concluding the evidence is insufficient and one concluding it supports use; its own conclusion is that there is "not yet a reliable evidence base" [50]. A meta-analysis of randomised trials found phonics instruction to be the only approach with statistically confirmed efficacy; coloured overlays did not reach significance [51]. A within-subject study of 61 children found no immediate benefit of Irlen overlays [53]. A joint statement of US paediatric and ophthalmology bodies says tinted filters lack scientific evidence and should not be recommended [52]. Evidence against a reliable benefit: strong.
- **The dissenting view.** Evans and Allen's review argues that a minority of people (about 20% of dyslexic readers) have "visual stress", distinct from dyslexia, and that individually prescribed tints improve their reading; they agree tints are "not a treatment for dyslexia" and call for larger trials [54]. The authors of [49] published a letter in response to that review (its text was not read) [54]. A further critical review calls the supporting results "at least controversial" and questions the syndrome itself [55]. The disagreement is live, and the balance of systematic reviews is against [50].
- **Coloured text.** Coloured-text research in dyslexia is sparse. One study found colouring whole words differently speeded reading and splitting words by colour slowed it, in dyslexic and non-dyslexic readers alike [32]. Background colour affects reading speed similarly in both groups [56].
- **BeeLine and dyslexia.** BeeLine's material says the approach benefits people with dyslexia and ADHD [59][4], supported by teacher reports [4][5] and user testimonials [2]. No controlled study in dyslexic readers was found. A study by a Harvard-Smithsonian laboratory was reported as planned in 2014 [13]; no published result was found.
- **Inference:** Buzzline should not claim that any colour scheme helps dyslexia or visual stress. The research gives no reason to prefer particular hues for dyslexic readers.

**The patent (US 9,026,907 B2, "Indicators of text continuity", N. Lum, granted 2015; description and claims read)** [1]:

- The independent claims cover formatting text so successive lines "have a continuity of color appearance across the line breaks" and "no continuity of color appearance between respective adjacent beginning portions of successive lines". They name no particular colours.
- Dependent claims cover gradual transitions along the line, including non-uniform rates and rates scaled to line length (claims 14–16), a line middle that differs from both ends (claim 17), colours at a line end and the next line start being "within a predetermined threshold distance of each other" (claim 18), and a two-line repeat (claim 20).
- The description's running example uses red and blue anchors with purple or pink between them. It says more than two anchors may be used and gives the reason for black anchors quoted in section 2.3.
- It suggests ambient light could select the palette: "subtle color changes", for example purple and blue anchors, in bright light, and "more dramatic color changes", for example red and blue, in low light. No evidence is offered for this.
- It describes a compatibility check that rejects settings which reduce readability, using a distance between text and background colour in HSV or HSL space or a sum of hex-value differences; the example is red-orange text on a red background.
- It notes that blue anchors collide with default link colour and suggests recolouring unvisited links.
- It allows attributes other than colour to carry the continuity (size, weight, shape, background, serif prominence).
- The description and claims contain no data on which colours work best and do not mention colour-vision deficiency.

## Candidate schemes

**Plain statement first: no research shows that any of these, or any existing Buzzline scheme, reads faster or is understood better than another.** Section 2.1 gives reason to expect no difference in reading speed among schemes that meet the same luminance contrast. The candidates are justified by who can see the cue (A and B) and by a weak comfort argument (C), not by reading performance.

### A · Kingfisher (blue and vermilion/orange) — chosen for colour-vision deficiency

- **What it is.** Light: Okabe–Ito blue unchanged, and Okabe–Ito vermilion `#d55e00` mixed 11% toward black to reach 4.5:1 on white (the published vermilion is 3.87:1). Dark: Okabe–Ito sky blue and orange unchanged [33].
- **Evidence it rests on.** The colours come from a palette designed to be unambiguous for protan, deutan and tritan viewers, pairing a cool with a warm colour as its authors advise, and using vermilion because pure red darkens for protanopes (section 3) [33]. Blue against orange differs along the blue-yellow dimension that red-green dichromats retain (section 3) [33][35]. In the simulation its worst anchor–accent figure on light pages is the best of any scheme, current or proposed (38 against 31 for the best current scheme), its two accents stay as far apart on light pages as `highcontrast`'s (48 against 49), and on dark pages they stay further apart than any current scheme's (51 against 40) (computed). It avoids red on dark surfaces, which WCAG and Okabe–Ito both advise against (section 2.4) [21][33]. Hue itself is not expected to cost reading speed (section 2.1) [15][16][17].
- **Also relevant, weak.** Sensitivity to red-green differences falls steeply away from the centre of gaze while blue-yellow sensitivity falls gradually, like luminance sensitivity [30], although colour detection remains possible far into the periphery with large enough stimuli [31]. **Inference:** since the next line's start is seen in peripheral vision before a return sweep, a cue that differs from the anchor in lightness and along blue-yellow may be easier to pick up than one that depends on red-green. Untested for reading.
- **Uncertain.** The palette was designed for telling categories apart in figures, not for body text, and was not validated on thin strokes. The light variant's accentB is no longer the published vermilion (ΔE00 5.9 from it, computed). On dark pages the worst anchor–accent figure (25, sky blue for protan viewers) is better than `classic` (20), `bumblebee` (22) and `ocean` (10) but below `highcontrast` (29). The two accents have nearly equal lightness in both variants, so telling accentA from accentB depends on hue alone. Light-variant accents sit near the 4.5:1 floor by design, to maximise their distance from black; they do not reach the 7:1 level WCAG associates with 20/80 acuity [21].

### B · Graphite (black to grey) — does not depend on colour vision

- **What it is.** One neutral accent used for both accent slots, giving a two-line cycle. Lightest greys that clear 4.5:1 with margin.
- **Evidence it rests on.** Legibility follows luminance contrast, not hue (section 2.1) [14][15][16][21]. A luminance-only cue is the same for every type of colour vision, including complete colour blindness, which WCAG's rationale gives as the reason to specify contrast by luminance [21]. Colour-deficient observers have normal or better light-dark sensitivity [38][21]. The one eye-tracking test of a line-start cue used a non-colour change (bold) and found fewer return-sweep errors (section 1) [9]. BeeLine has offered a grey-only setting [7], and the patent claims a two-line repeat and non-colour attributes (section 6) [1].
- **Uncertain.** Whether a lightness gradient alone is a strong enough cue is untested; the bold-word result [9] is the nearest evidence and is not the same manipulation. A two-line cycle cannot distinguish a line from the one two below it. Half of every line pair sits near the minimum contrast, and low-vision readers depend on high contrast [14][15], so this scheme is likely the worst choice for them (**inference**). A grey accent may read as "disabled" or secondary text; no source addresses this.

### C · Meadow (teal and amber) — weak evidence, optional

- **What it is.** Light: Okabe–Ito bluish green and orange, each mixed toward black until it clears 4.5:1 (18% and 33%). Dark: the published bluish green and orange [33].
- **Evidence it rests on.** It uses neither a saturated blue nor a red. The two ends of the spectrum are where the eye's focus differs most (about 1.8 dioptres apart) [22], where chromostereopsis arises [26][27], and where reading performance was depressed in the cases where wavelength mattered at all, with green and grey "nearly optimal" [17]. Bluish green was chosen by Okabe and Ito so as not to be confused with red or brown [33].
- **Why this is weak.** No study shows blue or red text slows reading at normal sizes (section 2.2) [17][18], and in Buzzline's layout the accents never sit side by side. For red-green dichromats the two accents converge in the simulation (36 → 15 and 20 on light pages), although both stay clear of the anchor (30 or more). The light-variant amber is heavily darkened and will look olive-brown, close to `bumblebee`'s adjusted gold. It shares its dark-variant orange with Kingfisher.
- **Recommendation.** Add only if a scheme without blue or red is wanted for its own sake.

### If only one scheme is added

Kingfisher has the clearest justification: a documented source for its colours, a stated purpose (colour-vision deficiency) that no current scheme was designed for, and the best simulated worst case on light pages. That is a claim about who can see the cue. It is not a claim that it reads faster.

A cheaper alternative that the simulation also points to (**inference**): leave the scheme list alone and adjust `ocean`'s dark teal, which is the single weakest colour found.

## What is not known

- Whether a line gradient makes adults read faster, skip fewer lines or understand more. No peer-reviewed adult study exists; the adult bold-cue study found no speed gain [9].
- Whether it helps dyslexic readers, readers with ADHD or low-vision readers. Claims exist [2][4][59]; controlled studies were not found.
- Whether any palette outperforms another. No comparison has been published, including for the candidates above.
- How large a colour or lightness difference between anchor and accent is needed for the cue to work in thin text seen in peripheral vision. The ΔE00 figures here rank schemes; they do not establish a threshold.
- How people with milder, anomalous forms of colour deficiency, whom Okabe and Ito describe as seeing colours more like people with typical vision [33], experience these schemes. The simulation covers only full dichromacy.
- Whether a lightness-only gradient works as a cue (Graphite).
- Whether saturated red or blue accents are less comfortable over long reading sessions or on dark pages. Optics suggest a possible effect [22][42]; reading studies have not shown one [17][18].
- Whether "halation" of light text on dark backgrounds is a measurable effect, and for whom.
- Whether a gradient's novelty wears off, or whether habitual use changes reading of plain text; both are raised as open questions in [7].
- What colours BeeLine's extension currently ships beyond the three demo themes.
